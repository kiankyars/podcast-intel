## TL;DR

- **The founders position Supabase Compute as the missing runtime for long-lived agents and background work beside Postgres, rather than treating Edge Functions as sufficient for every workload.** [00:06:45] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 06:45–07:26 they describe full containers, arbitrary languages and direct database connections on the same private network; at 54:03–54:22 they call it a primitive for future durable workflows. The [October 27 launch](https://supabase.com/blog/supabase-compute-private-alpha) confirms private alpha in us-west-2, for evaluation only: services may restart, receive all project secrets, and have no autoscaling, GPU, persistent disk or agent sandboxes. State must be persisted separately. The interview’s two-second Edge limit refers to CPU time; [current limits](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/functions/limits) permit 150/400 seconds of worker wall time on free/paid plans. This is not a finished durable-execution guarantee.
- **The founders plan a smaller Spark instance using Turso’s architecture because an agent-created prototype should not require its own dedicated Postgres machine.** [00:15:52] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 12:44 they report roughly five million database launches per month; at 15:33–17:01 they describe Spark, library compatibility and a desired smooth upgrade path to Postgres. The [October 2 Turso announcement](https://supabase.com/blog/supabase-is-acquiring-turso) separately reports more than one million launches weekly and describes many SQLite databases sharing a server, loading on demand and suspending when idle. These counts are creations, not active paying customers. Billions per month is their scenario, not achieved capacity. Spark availability, complete Postgres compatibility and zero-downtime upgrades are not established by the announcement.
- **Boudreaux and Predd propose evaluating human agency across an actual deployment and over time, because task benchmarks and isolated approvals can miss a gradual transfer of decision authority to AI.** [1:03:13; 1:03:53] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/freedom-of-action-america-s-asi-strategy-w-rand-s-joel-predd-ben-boudreaux-matt-chessen/)). They describe mapping who makes consequential decisions, then tracking whether AI changes participation, persuades decision makers or sets the agenda they consider. Individually reasonable delegations can accumulate into a loss of control that a single decision review would not expose. Boudreaux cites model deployment and fab development as illustrative decisions and says the research is ongoing with a paper planned; no validated agency benchmark or measured result is presented.

## More findings

- **Copplestone’s Git-first schema strategy gives coding agents a readable desired database state, reducing the need to reconstruct it from a long migration history.** [01:00:15] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 58:47–01:00:37 he explains file-based tables, policies and comments, backed by the new pg-delta library. The [October 13 technical post](https://supabase.com/blog/declarative-schemas-2-0-pg-delta) confirms expanded support for policies, grants and other objects. The alpha engine compares catalogs in two temporary databases and still generates ordinary SQL migrations for review and application; it does not eliminate migrations or their execution. It excludes application-data changes and storage buckets, and does not orchestrate zero-downtime rollout. The benefit is clearer context and broader schema coverage, not autonomous safe production changes.
- **The founders report that agent-driven acquisition increased both signup activation and paid conversion, contradicting their expectation that a broader, more casual funnel would dilute conversion.** [00:32:57] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 32:24–33:26 they say signups accelerated as models improved, while activation and conversion to pay also rose; they say these users converted to pay more often than traditional developers. Their explanation is that an agent completes setup in one step. No cohort sizes, activation definition, paid-conversion percentages, retention or revenue mix are disclosed. The near-100% activation remark is therefore not treated as a measured universal rate, and model improvement is their causal interpretation rather than a controlled result. Swyx’s favorable AEO ranking is excluded as independent corroboration given his disclosed investment and sponsorship ties.
- **Copplestone describes compensating sales for account expansion above a baseline, with outreach triggered by actual product problems and AI-assisted preparation for support calls.** [00:43:03] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 42:01–43:35 he describes usage limits, errors and account growth triggering outreach, then reports 25% monthly growth among nonresponders versus 125% among accounts receiving help. He says compensation targets the incremental difference. That is a 100-percentage-point difference in growth rates, not proof of a 100% causal sales effect: customers self-select by responding, and the interview supplies no randomization or matched baseline. His 1,000-outreach/500-response example is illustrative, not an audited sample. The concrete finding is incentive design and problem-led selling, not experimentally established uplift.
- **The founders have prioritized reliable multi-node operation in Multigres before transparent sharding, separating present failover work from the larger scaling promise.** [00:09:53] ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/supabase)). At 09:27–10:13 they report 1,000 sequential failovers without data loss; the test setup and independent results are not supplied. At 11:23–11:38 they explicitly say sharding is unreleased and tentatively expect some work early next year. [Hosted Multigres documentation](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/multigres) confirms private-alpha failover and pooling, with no uptime SLA or production target. Sharding, Realtime, point-in-time recovery and combination with OrioleDB are unavailable in this stage. The reported stress test does not establish that data loss is impossible or that every competing operator is unsafe.
- **Chessen reports that RAND has assembled a 106-capability security catalog but is still estimating costs, lead times and ownership; its immediate recommendation is to build capabilities shared across strategies and those that cannot be improvised quickly.** [1:06:42; 1:13:05; 1:14:09; 1:19:40] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/freedom-of-action-america-s-asi-strategy-w-rand-s-joel-predd-ben-boudreaux-matt-chessen/)). He prioritizes visibility into model capability and compute, plus long-lead verification infrastructure that could support a later policy shift. The team plans a research sprint over the next couple of months to identify approximate costs, build times and responsible actors. Predd explicitly says cost estimates are not done, so the host's 1.5-times-cost comparison is not a budget finding. Later, Chessen explains that credible independent evaluation and verification can support both deterrence and reciprocal cooperation without committing to either strategy.
- **Boudreaux argues that operational independence from AI is part of credible control: shutdown authority and technical switches are insufficient when using them would disable essential services.** [59:21; 1:25:05; 1:26:22; 1:27:13; 1:28:00] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/freedom-of-action-america-s-asi-strategy-w-rand-s-joel-predd-ben-boudreaux-matt-chessen/)). He links the feasibility of a future moratorium to society's ability to operate without advanced AI. For incidents, he calls for technical shutdown capability, legal authority and rehearsed crisis processes, backed by dependency mapping, manual operation and practiced human fallbacks. This is a proposed resilience mechanism, not a claim that model deterrence has been demonstrated. Separately, Labenz reports a limited personal example: an agent rotated his console credentials and briefly locked him out, after which he recovered access by asking through a terminal; he does not describe malicious intent.
- **Chessen warns that strategies centered on one or two controllable superintelligent systems may fail under distributed agent ecosystems, including the possibility of stronger aggregate behavior from individually weaker agents.** [1:32:14] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/freedom-of-action-america-s-asi-strategy-w-rand-s-joel-predd-ben-boudreaux-matt-chessen/)). He says the strategies RAND reviewed became brittle when considering many distributed systems, narrow systems with architectural constraints, or swarms below individual AGI or ASI capability. He identifies interactions, coalitions and emergent behavior as under-researched. This challenges the assumption that governing a few frontier laboratories captures all relevant risk; it is a research concern rather than evidence that such swarms have achieved superintelligence, and the interview supplies no measured swarm result.

### [Why Agents Love Supabase](https://latent.space/p/supabase)

Latent Space · Relevance 4/5

Supabase founders Paul Copplestone and Ant Wilson explain how agent-generated applications are changing both database economics and developer-tool distribution: many more small databases, longer-running compute beside the data, and schemas agents can inspect in Git. They report improving paid conversion despite a broader user funnel, and describe compensating sales for account growth above an observed baseline. Product checks distinguish available features from previews: Compute and hosted Multigres remain private alpha; Spark and transparent sharding are future ambitions. Swyx discloses being a seed investor and a Supabase sponsorship relationship. The complete interview closes at 01:16:28, consistent with the 76:38 RSS duration. RSS dates the episode October 26; the publisher page shows October 28. Its pre-recorded Select announcements concern the October 2 conference, not new October 29 launches.

**Why it matters:** The consequential change is the cost and friction of creating and operating each application. Agent adoption can expand a backend’s funnel and automate activation, but serving millions of small projects requires a different architecture from scaling one large transactional database. The founders supply useful operating observations; conversion, sales uplift and reliability claims remain company-reported rather than independently measured outcomes.

### [Freedom of Action: America's ASI Strategy, w/ RAND's Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux & Matt Chessen](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/freedom-of-action-america-s-asi-strategy-w-rand-s-joel-predd-ben-boudreaux-matt-chessen/)

The Cognitive Revolution · Relevance 3/5

RAND's Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux and Matt Chessen argue that governments and companies should build the capabilities needed to change AI strategy before a crisis forces the choice. The useful additions are deployment-level evaluations of human agency, a still-unpriced catalog of 106 security capabilities, and human fallback capacity that makes shutting down AI practically possible. These are research proposals and the authors' judgments, not adopted U.S. policy. Their independently initiated September 2026 [RAND paper](https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PEA5105-1.html) corroborates the emphasis on long-lead capabilities, verification and preserving strategic options. The publisher's automatic transcript identifies all three guests and reaches the interview farewell at 1:46:33; it abbreviates ads, does not transcribe the interval before the 1:48:52 outro, and includes trailing website text, which was excluded.

**Why it matters:** This reframes AI governance as an engineering and institutional preparation problem with expiring options. A nominal human approval step does not demonstrate retained control, and the ability to switch off a model is of limited use if an organization cannot function without it. The practical implications concern evaluation design, permissions, incident response and operational continuity. The authors have not yet established the program's costs, implementation schedule or effectiveness, and the conversation supplies no evidence that a particular superintelligence strategy has been adopted or that distributed agents have already achieved superintelligence.
