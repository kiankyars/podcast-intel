### [Simulation: the new Scaling Law — Joon Sung Park, Simile AI](https://www.latent.space/p/simile)

**Latent Space | relevance 5/5 | [local summary](../episodes/2026-08-21/simulation-the-new-scaling-law-joon-sung-park-simile-ai-392efcaf08295a0dda16/summary.md)**

Joon Sung Park describes Simile as a behavioral-model company whose differentiation is not a larger prompted persona layer but post-training on consented interviews, observed behavior, transactions, and causally informative randomized trials. He grounds that thesis in a 1,000-person research design whose digital twins reproduced behavior and attitudes 85% as accurately as people reproduced their own responses, then says generic frontier models fall to 20-30% on some niche-population tasks and roughly 50-60% on general-population tasks. Commercially, Simile recruits targeted populations, maintains population- and individual-level models, and applies them to experiments, product testing, and synthetic panels. Park also reports an early, still-undisclosed scaling relationship between human data, compute, and simulation quality. The research design and first-party operating details are unusually consequential, but the episode provides no scaling curve, benchmark specification for the frontier-model comparison, customer outcome metric, or independent audit of commercial scale.

**Why it matters:** The episode supports a distinct AI scaling and moat thesis: accurate human simulation may depend less on larger general-purpose models than on proprietary causal-behavior data, representative subpopulations, and evaluations calibrated to human test-retest consistency. If the reported gains hold prospectively, synthetic populations could compress product research and expose second-order effects before expensive decisions are deployed. The investment case remains evidence-sensitive, however: Simile's strongest commercial and scaling claims are self-reported, and deployment in policy or other high-stakes settings would make consent, representation, drift, and manipulation controls central product constraints.

### [AI in the AM — Weekly Highlights: Relaunch Week (Aug 17–20, 2026)](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-in-the-am-weekly-highlights-relaunch-week-aug-17-20-2026/)

**The Cognitive Revolution | relevance 5/5 | [local summary](../episodes/2026-08-22/ai-in-the-am-weekly-highlights-relaunch-week-aug-17-20-2026-77e4ba8b8b10a6e4ea71/summary.md)**

This compilation ties frontier-agent failures to concrete monitoring gaps, then follows the economics of deploying agents through model routing, inference capacity, supervision, and accelerator software. The strongest material is first-party: Adam Gleave describes incident-detection and audit-access failures; Adam Wenchel and Jonathan Cornelissen quantify why cheaper open-weight models still do not automatically unlock broad deployment; Mitchell Troyanovsky explains action-level supervision for long-running accounting agents; and Jay Dawani argues that memory, networking, power, and software scheduling now matter more than another hand-tuned kernel. A secondary but consequential segment also presents Prakash's reading of Anthropic's redacted internal-model results, which should be treated as an interpretation pending direct review of the report.

**Why it matters:** The episode supplies a connected view of the current agent stack: capability is outrunning evaluation monitoring, independent auditors remain dependent on lab access, application economics can justify model substitution but serving capacity can block it, and physical power scarcity raises the value of routing and utilization software. Those mechanisms affect risk assumptions about unreleased frontier systems, margins for agent companies, the practical competitiveness of open-weight models, and the durability of Nvidia's software ecosystem.
