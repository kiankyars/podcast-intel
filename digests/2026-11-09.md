Moving beyond frontier APIs into post-training is an exploration for cost and latency, not a reported economic breakthrough.** [00:52:33; 00:53:44; 00:54:15; 00:54:23; 00:56:06; 00:58:19; 00:58:53] ([Latent Space](https://lspace.beehiiv.com/podcast/s/latent_space_the_ai_engineer_podcast/one_million_ai_engineer_special_how_stanford_teaches_ai-native_engineering_mihail_eric_monaco)). After starting with frontier-model APIs, he says the team is in the phase of exploring open-weight models, RL environments and post-training. He supplies no production model identity, benchmark, realized savings or gross-margin result. Separate later context: Eric’s October 30 company article describes domain experts building agent workflows and evaluation rubrics, consistent with the organizational approach in the interview: [Principles of Effective Vertical AI](https://www.monaco.com/blog/principles-of-effective-vertical-ai).
- **The discussion separates two constraints on autonomous development: making each change verifiable, and retaining the system’s intended behavior across changes. Better agents alone do not resolve either organizational requirement.** [01:06:27; 01:12:11; 01:12:21; 01:12:56; 01:16:19; 01:17:09; 01:17:48] ([Latent Space](https://lspace.beehiiv.com/podcast/s/latent_space_the_ai_engineer_podcast/one_million_ai_engineer_special_how_stanford_teaches_ai-native_engineering_mihail_eric_monaco)). Eric says companies he talks to are blocked by CI/CD, merge queues and slow application setup, and treats a verifiable, understandable codebase as a prerequisite for parallel agents. Monaco uses detailed Linear issues and PR descriptions as local specifications. Swyx challenges whether those preserve global intent when a later change reverses an earlier decision; Eric points to versioned intent elsewhere rather than claiming Monaco has solved this. The opportunity for persistent intent and verification systems is an inference from that gap, not a measured deployment result. The discussion’s 30-plus PRs per engineer per day is hypothetical, not Monaco throughput.
- **Doshi says Google evaluates the complete served experience because judge errors, production filters and serving choices can change what users get from the same model.** [00:28:37; 00:28:45; 00:28:52; 00:29:04; 00:29:12; 00:29:21; 00:29:28; 00:29:31; 00:29:38; 00:29:44] ([Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-it-actually-takes-to-build-frontier)). She identifies judge quality as a separate dependency of evaluation accuracy, then names production mitigations, filters and serving as factors that can alter performance. Her audio-team example is concrete: the words can be good while voice naturalness, tenor and inflection are affected by serving. This extends earlier harness-sensitive benchmark coverage to perceptual output quality and the evaluation system itself. The engineering implication is to validate judges and test the production path, including audio, rather than infer user experience from checkpoint-only results. She gives no measured degradation or identified serving component, so the magnitude and specific failure mechanism remain unspecified.
- **Doshi warns that customers can constrain improved models with systems built around older weaknesses, and recommends repeated testing alongside a capability-based portfolio of product bets.** [00:33:29; 00:33:50; 00:34:05; 00:34:14; 00:34:23; 00:34:32; 00:35:00; 00:35:07; 00:35:47; 00:35:54; 00:36:23; 00:36:32] ([Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-it-actually-takes-to-build-frontier)). She says some customers have built rigid systems around earlier models and that forcing a new model into that structure may hamper its output. Her recommendation is to retest; the host’s suggestion to periodically remove all skills is not presented as a proven Google practice. Her related 60-20-20 heuristic allocates a functional prototype across features that work now, features that nearly work, and more ambitious capabilities showing early promise. She explicitly calls the percentages flexible and says she cannot reliably predict exactly where models will be three months ahead. This is advice for choosing and reassessing product experiments, not a 60% production success target, an official Google budget or a release timetable.
- **Doshi identifies chain-of-thought monitoring and intervention as a practical layer in Google’s deployment safety approach, with confidence conditional on continued scrutiny.** [00:10:48; 00:10:54; 00:11:03; 00:11:07; 00:11:15; 00:11:22; 00:11:33; 00:13:12; 00:13:18; 00:13:29; 00:13:39] ([Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-it-actually-takes-to-build-frontier)). She describes monitors examining reasoning for departures from directed actions and enabling intervention, rather than claiming that model training eliminates misalignment. Separate corroboration, predating the interview’s November 8 publication: Google’s [October 13 Gemini 4 Argon safety post](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/gemini-4-model-safety-security/) says its monitor reads both actions and reasoning, blocks severe boundary violations and flags serious internal cases for people. Google reports using it in products, internal coding agents, training runs and capability evaluations. The same post says it avoids penalizing reasoning text during training to preserve monitorability. These are Google’s reported controls, not an independent estimate of detection coverage; the interview supplies no false-positive rate, missed-event rate or latency overhead. Her optimism is explicitly tied to people continuing to investigate risks.

### [Why PMs are suddenly valuable again | Tulsee Doshi (Head of Product, Gemini)](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-it-actually-takes-to-build-frontier)

Lenny's Podcast · Relevance 3/5

Tulsee Doshi explains how Gemini product decisions enter model training: user feedback becomes a behavioral specification, evaluation rubrics, data and reward-modeling inputs. She also describes why judge quality and the production serving stack can change the experience users receive, especially in audio. Her advice for builders is to plan around demonstrated and emerging capabilities and repeatedly test whether existing scaffolding still helps. The useful contribution is a first-party account of model-development and deployment practices, rather than a new capability or product-launch announcement. Published November 8, the interview treats Argon availability as a future expectation at recording; the exact recording date is not supplied.

**Why it matters:** Model behavior, evaluation and deployment are coupled engineering choices. A good checkpoint can still produce a poor product if the judge rewards the wrong behavior, serving changes the audio experience, or inherited instructions constrain a stronger model. Doshi gives specific interfaces between product and research work, while her roadmap percentages and safety confidence remain judgments rather than measured performance guarantees. The broad argument for building ahead of model improvements overlaps October 12 coverage of Josh Woodward; the added detail is how to test and revise the surrounding system.
