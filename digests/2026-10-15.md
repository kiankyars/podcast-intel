## Episodes

### [Lab-in-the-Loop at Compute Speed — Ian Quigley, Leash Bio](https://www.latent.space/p/lab-in-the-loop-at-compute-speed)

Latent Space · Relevance 4/5

Leash Bio co-founder Ian Quigley describes how dense, deliberately varied protein–molecule measurements support both binding models and a fast experimental sandbox for reasoning agents. The most consequential material is his account of hidden chemical-library generalization failures, chemist-authorship confounding in public benchmarks, and physical protein mutations used to challenge memorization. He also reports early agent-search and mouse pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic results, with substantial limits on extrapolation. The publisher says this conversation was recorded roughly two months before its October 14 publication; the later Minos release is separate evidence.

**Why it matters:** A plausible path to scientific agent training is to measure a bounded experimental landscape once and reuse its outcomes for many adaptive search trajectories. Leash's experience also argues that data diversity, meaningful negatives, and evaluation splits can matter more than model complexity or raw measurement counts. Binding rankings and replayed searches remain far upstream of reliable absolute affinity, off-library discoveries, safety, and clinical benefit.

### [Governing AI Agents: Alan Chan of GovAI on Monitoring, Agent IDs, Rogue Agents & Preparing for RSI](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/governing-ai-agents-alan-chan-of-govai-on-monitoring-agent-ids-rogue-agents-preparing-for-rsi/)

The Cognitive Revolution · Relevance 4/5

GovAI researcher Alan Chan identifies internal training and evaluation as a potentially underprotected part of the agent lifecycle, and proposes synchronous monitoring, traceable agent identities and explicit incident-triggered pauses. On automated AI R&D, he argues that code volume and experiment counts are insufficient evidence of accelerating progress: labs need longitudinal productivity measurements, resource inputs and tests of whether safety research and oversight keep pace. He expects persistent rogue agents to emerge but distinguishes that forecast from demonstrated incidents and remains skeptical of near-term full takeover. The episode supplies concrete governance proposals and a useful measurement framework, rather than new capability results.

**Why it matters:** For frontier labs and agent-platform operators, the practical issue is whether controls cover internal research as rigorously as customer products. For assessments of recursive self-improvement, Chan raises the evidentiary bar from more automated activity to greater capability gains per unit of research input, with oversight measured alongside throughput. His proposals imply demand for identity, monitoring and independent-audit infrastructure, but do not establish product adoption or new legal requirements.

### [OpenAI Uncovers Spy Device, Apple x LG, Home Devices Tier List | Thomas Neckel, Kabir Jain, Danielle Axman, John Urschel, Nicolas Sharp, Jason Ballard, Erik Bernhardsson, Josh Tyrangiel, Aida Baradari](https://share.transistor.fm/s/6209dfad)

TBPN · Relevance 4/5

The useful material is in the founder and operator interviews: Modal describes capacity constraints across roughly 20–25 clouds; Fragment and Attio are adapting financial and customer systems for agent access with explicit controls; ICON is expanding from building projects itself to supplying builders with an integrated construction system. Erewhon supplies a concrete example of a retailer investing in its own software and customer relationships. Host speculation, sponsor copy, and uncorroborated security allegations are excluded.

**Why it matters:** These interviews suggest that agent adoption is creating demand for infrastructure that combines context, deterministic enforcement, and reliable execution. Fragment and Attio illustrate where application vendors may retain value as models improve; Modal illustrates the operational complexity and capital needed beneath agent workloads. ICON's commercialization and Erewhon's internal software investment offer useful comparisons for how technology changes operating models, while neither interview establishes independently measured productivity gains.
