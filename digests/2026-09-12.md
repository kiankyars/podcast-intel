# 2026-09-12

## TL;DR

- **O’Neill reports that repeated small-data model updates still cause forgetting and general-capability degradation, even when on-policy distillation postpones the failure.** [00:45:24] ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)). Discussing research his team and others have done, O’Neill contrasts workable large-batch mid-training with hundreds of narrow micro-updates: supervised tuning on successful traces loses earlier adaptations and broader capabilities, while on-policy distillation extends but does not eliminate the problem. He says RL is better at adding capabilities than explicit company-specific knowledge. Millidge argues that training a fresh model with the accumulated data can work better, suggesting a plasticity and distribution-shift problem rather than simply insufficient capacity. No model sizes, retention curves or controlled comparisons are provided.
- **Schulman argues that access to a strong teacher is insufficient for useful distillation unless the prompt distribution exercises realistic, multi-turn work.** [00:18:39] ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)). He distinguishes environment difficulty from realism: hard, verifiable puzzles can improve benchmarks without reproducing coding sessions involving changing human requests and multiple objectives. His hypothesis is that larger models transfer better from narrow tasks, while a student trained on similarly narrow prompts can match benchmark scores but miss the teacher’s practical behavior. He explicitly does not know competitors’ post-training details. The implication is that realistic interaction data, not just teacher access or more difficult tasks, can differentiate distilled models.
- **Patel reports a small-scale recipe-by-dataset experiment finding roughly 12.0× compute-efficiency gains from data changes versus 3.7× from model-recipe improvements.** [01:00:33] ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)). Patel says he and Jerry Han crossed older and newer training recipes with older and newer datasets. Their [September 8 methods](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data) confirm these factors at a 1e19-FLOP budget using OLMES evaluations and a fixed 2,048-token context; the model-recipe axis includes optimizer and training changes, not architecture alone. Patel stresses the small scale. Millidge cautions that architecture can unlock capabilities such as long context that a fixed short-context comparison misses. The authors also flag evaluation noise and limited hyperparameter tuning. The study has not been independently reproduced here, and the ratio is not established at frontier scale.

## More findings

- **Millidge and O’Neill explain recent RL gains as a combination of strong mid-training, wider task coverage and longer-horizon persistence, rather than uniform reasoning transfer across domains.** [01:18:03] ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)). Millidge says synthetic reasoning mid-training supplies much of the starting behavior, leaving RL to adjust a policy with a concentrated success signal instead of matching every token of another model’s reasoning. O’Neill distinguishes learning to keep working productively for longer from acquiring all the reasoning skills of a new domain; math training does not remove the need for coding environments. Millidge allows some cross-domain transfer and emphasizes the much broader set of tasks labs now train on. They offer a mechanistic interpretation, not a controlled attribution of all recent model gains.
- **The researchers identify choosing and revising research objectives as a distinct bottleneck that faster experiment execution does not automatically solve.** [00:00:00; 00:28:06] ([Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)). O’Neill separates optimizing an already specified objective from discovering the next useful objective or paradigm. Millidge says current models propose many small, miscellaneous experiments and that a self-contained loop must repeatedly select objectives without drifting off course. Schulman describes research as turning an intuition into a simplified test, then restoring realism, and expects researcher feedback plus practice environments to train successive model generations. Millidge counters that verifiable environments can exceed human performance, but the group does not establish that this transfers to autonomous, open-ended research.
- **Mozilla CTO Raffi Krikorian says Firefox has reached diminishing returns on the bugs its current Mythos access finds, while estimating full-codebase security runs would cost hundreds of thousands of dollars without supplied credits.** [52:48; 54:24; 55:05; 55:13] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)). Krikorian says Mozilla rapidly worked through Mythos findings, sometimes using the model to build a reproduction or testing harness rather than directly fix a bug. He explicitly avoids claiming all bugs are gone. Mozilla has received model access and credits; his hypothetical run cost is a back-of-envelope estimate, and he agrees repeated sweeps at the pace of new models could make that a monthly expense. He identifies slower-remediating infrastructure operators as a bigger concern than Firefox, not evidence of a specific breach.
- **Krikorian describes two different code-ownership contracts inside Mozilla: human review and commitment for Firefox, versus spec-driven regeneration of some entire codebases at Mozilla AI.** [55:38; 58:17] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)). He says Firefox allows agent-written code but requires a human to review, understand and stand behind each commit. At Mozilla AI, he reports some codebases have no human-written implementation: versioned specifications drive automated regeneration, with unit and end-to-end tests remaining green even as implementation bytes change frequently. Those tests establish the reported acceptance criterion, not a proof that regenerated software is correct or secure. Earlier, he describes CQ as a way to share agent discoveries and team workflow rules rather than have teammates independently recreate the same systems.
- **Baseten CTO Amir Haghighat says roughly 90% of revenue comes from serving customers' custom models, although adoption of its standard open-model APIs is increasing.** [1:04:14] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)). Haghighat distinguishes customers that pretrain their own models, including Poolside, Inception and Cartesia, from customers that post-train existing models. He says the latter group also brings substantial validation work. He reports an uptick in the vanilla open-model API product after open models became useful for longer agent tasks, identifying June and GLM 5.2 as his perceived turning point. The revenue mix and adoption trend are company-reported; he supplies no absolute revenue, growth rate or profitability figures.
- **Haghighat separates the tenant-isolation boundary Baseten provides from the agent-behavior safeguards it currently leaves largely to customers and evaluation partners.** [1:02:22; 1:04:14] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)). He contrasts a basic container sandbox with VM or microVM isolation intended to keep one customer's code from accessing another customer's data. He does not claim this controls what the agent tries to do inside that boundary. Customers currently rely on their own model validation and partners such as Braintrust and LangChain; some accept single-tenant execution and blocked outbound networking. He expects a broader guardrail offering over time but describes it as a scope and prioritization decision, not an already-delivered end-to-end safety guarantee.
- **Snorbl CEO Mike Rizkalla reports about $20,000 per hour of authored content for its children's companion, using constrained language understanding and contextual triggers instead of open-ended generated dialogue.** [1:06:59; 1:07:55] ([The Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)). Rizkalla describes a small language model with a fixed intended role, a system for producing content with subject-matter experts, and keyword interpretation conditioned on context. He says parent-approved progression unlocks material as a child develops. His cost figure is content-production cost, not the price of an hour of customer usage. The approach deliberately purchases editorial control through authored material; proposed specialized family content and emotion-sensitive features are expansion plans, not demonstrated therapeutic effects or a blanket safety guarantee.

### [AI researchers debate how close we are to recursive self-improvement](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie)

Dwarkesh Podcast · Relevance 5/5

John Schulman, Beren Millidge and Charlie O’Neill separate strong coding and benchmark progress from the harder requirements for autonomous research: choosing useful objectives, transferring into realistic work and learning repeatedly without forgetting. O’Neill describes failures in small, iterative model updates; Schulman explains why realistic prompts matter for distillation; and Patel reports a small-scale experiment in which improved datasets contributed more compute efficiency than training-recipe changes. Their explanations of RL progress emphasize strong starting models, broader task coverage and longer task horizons, not a demonstrated self-improving research loop.

**Why it matters:** The discussion identifies concrete tests for both frontier-model differentiation and application-specific learning: realistic multi-turn performance, retention after repeated updates and independent selection of useful experiments. It also cautions against extrapolating small-model efficiency measurements or longer coding horizons into general research automation. The transcript supplies chapter-level timestamps rather than timestamps for individual remarks.

### [AI:AM Highlights: Astra as AGI, OpenAI's Pause, Mythos @ Mozilla & Human Agency vs Technocapitalism](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/ai-am-highlights-astra-as-agi-openai-s-pause-mythos-mozilla-human-agency-vs-technocapitalism/)

The Cognitive Revolution · Relevance 4/5

The strongest material is first-party operating detail from Mozilla, Baseten and Snorbl, rather than the hosts' AGI and training-pause debate. Mozilla describes diminishing returns from one generation of security models and sharply different rules for agent-written code. Baseten distinguishes infrastructure isolation from agent-behavior safety while reporting that custom-model serving still supplies about 90% of revenue. Snorbl puts an explicit production cost on authored children's content instead of open-ended generation.

**Why it matters:** These deployments expose costs and responsibilities hidden by capability demonstrations: repeated vulnerability sweeps require substantial inference budgets, passing tests does not settle human ownership of code, and an isolated runtime does not make an agent's actions safe. Baseten's revenue mix also cautions against treating the company as primarily a generic open-model API reseller, while Snorbl illustrates a content-production business model with deliberately constrained AI behavior.

### [Fruitfly Hard Takeoff, Washington on AI Risk, 𝕏 Timeline Reactions | Thijs Simonian, Alex Heath & Guy Oseary, Mitesh Agrawal](https://share.transistor.fm/s/e0816bdf)

TBPN · Relevance 4/5

OpenAI robotics intern Thijs Simonian describes a painting robot that balances slow visual reasoning with longer code-based action plans and background monitoring. Positron's Mitesh Agrawal explains the separate hurdles of memory supply, fabrication scale, deployment financing, and software optimization for inference hardware, distinguishing its shipping FPGA product from its planned custom silicon.

**Why it matters:** The robotics demo illustrates a concrete way to manage expensive perception loops without claiming real-time general autonomy. Positron's account shows why a working inference architecture and rapid model port are only starting points: scalable component supply, deployable infrastructure, financing, and workload-specific efficiency still determine commercial adoption.
