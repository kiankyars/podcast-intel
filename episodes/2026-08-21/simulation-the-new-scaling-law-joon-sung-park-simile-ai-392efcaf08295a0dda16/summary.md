# Simulation: the new Scaling Law — Joon Sung Park, Simile AI

- Podcast: Latent Space
- Published: 2026-08-21
- Source: https://www.latent.space/p/simile
- Relevance: 5/5

Joon Sung Park describes Simile as a behavioral-model company whose differentiation is not a larger prompted persona layer but post-training on consented interviews, observed behavior, transactions, and causally informative randomized trials. He grounds that thesis in a 1,000-person research design whose digital twins reproduced behavior and attitudes 85% as accurately as people reproduced their own responses, then says generic frontier models fall to 20-30% on some niche-population tasks and roughly 50-60% on general-population tasks. Commercially, Simile recruits targeted populations, maintains population- and individual-level models, and applies them to experiments, product testing, and synthetic panels. Park also reports an early, still-undisclosed scaling relationship between human data, compute, and simulation quality. The research design and first-party operating details are unusually consequential, but the episode provides no scaling curve, benchmark specification for the frontier-model comparison, customer outcome metric, or independent audit of commercial scale.

**Why it matters:** The episode supports a distinct AI scaling and moat thesis: accurate human simulation may depend less on larger general-purpose models than on proprietary causal-behavior data, representative subpopulations, and evaluations calibrated to human test-retest consistency. If the reported gains hold prospectively, synthetic populations could compress product research and expose second-order effects before expensive decisions are deployed. The investment case remains evidence-sensitive, however: Simile's strongest commercial and scaling claims are self-reported, and deployment in policy or other high-stakes settings would make consent, representation, drift, and manipulation controls central product constraints.

## Changed Views Or Tensions

- Behavioral simulation appears to be a distinct post-training problem rather than a prompt-engineering extension of frontier LLMs; reproducing irrationality and population-specific errors is part of the target.
- The reported 85% result should be read as performance normalized to human self-consistency, not as raw prediction accuracy; that distinction makes the claim more interpretable but still requires benchmark-level scrutiny.
- Simile's potential moat is concentrated in consented causal-behavior data, representative subpopulation coverage, and reusable person models rather than in synthetic persona count alone.
- The commercial product today is closer to a scalable replacement or extension for human panels and product testing than to the proposed eight-billion-person world simulation.
- The claimed simulation scaling law is potentially important but remains an internal observation until Simile publishes curves, held-out evaluations, and marginal returns to data and compute.

## Follow-Ups

- Audit the 1,000-person paper's exact normalization, confidence intervals, subgroup performance, leakage controls, and baseline models before using the 85% result in a commercial forecast.
- Locate the randomized-trial follow-up paper and quantify the lift from RCT post-training relative to interview-only, observational-data, and frontier-model baselines.
- Request Simile's scaling curves with model size, unique participant count, compute, held-out domains, and diminishing returns separated; test whether gains persist on prospective interventions.
- Verify the reported weekly collection scale, panel reach, production simulation volume, and enterprise deployments, distinguishing accessible panelists from deeply modeled people and paid query volume.
- Track prospective customer outcomes from Wealthfront, Gallup, or another named deployment against live human panels and realized behavior rather than retrospective fit.
- Review consent, compensation, opt-out, demographic weighting, model drift, and restrictions on political or manipulative use before treating high-stakes simulation as deployable.
