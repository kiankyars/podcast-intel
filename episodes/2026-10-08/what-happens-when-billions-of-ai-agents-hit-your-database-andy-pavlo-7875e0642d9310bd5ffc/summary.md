# What Happens When Billions of AI Agents Hit Your Database? (Andy Pavlo)

- Podcast: The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
- Published: 2026-10-08
- Source: https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/firstmark/episodes/What-Happens-When-Billions-of-AI-Agents-Hit-Your-Database--Andy-Pavlo-e3q4qu7
- Relevance: 4/5

Andy Pavlo, CMU database researcher and recently appointed head of ClickHouse Labs, argues that agents first change database volume, development workflows and operating costs; a fundamentally new database architecture is still an open research question. He reports rapid, useful LLM tuning and widespread disclosed coding-agent contributions, while separating those advances from production correctness. His most striking text-to-SQL example is an unnamed bank that reportedly spent years and millions of dollars building semantic context. GPU acceleration and combining Postgres with analytics offer concrete architectural opportunities, but workload economics, product maturity and organizational ownership still matter.

**Why it matters:** The useful investment and engineering distinction is between agents increasing demand for existing database capabilities and proving a new engine is necessary. Pavlo identifies near-term value in semantic context, isolated database branches, permissions, tuning and integrated developer workflows. His numbers have different evidentiary strength: tuning is a first-party research impression, bank accuracy is secondhand, disclosed AI commits measure project adoption, and 10–100× query growth is a forecast. Pavlo now works for ClickHouse, and host Matt Turck discloses that FirstMark invests in the company.

## Changed Views Or Tensions

- Text-to-SQL readiness depends on the business semantics and evaluation surrounding the model. The bank anecdote raises the possibility of high accuracy on a curated workload while increasing the expected implementation cost; it does not establish general 99.5% reliability.
- The value of LLM tuning may come first from quickly fixing poor defaults, with more specialized search reserved for expensive edge cases. A useful recommendation is different from a safely autonomous production tuning loop.
- Database creation counts, query volume and revenue should be modeled separately. Cheap, ephemeral development branches can proliferate without proportional persistent storage or paid analytical usage.
- GPU acceleration is increasingly an execution-layer option inside an existing database workflow, but the decisive comparison remains end-to-end workload economics and supported operations.
- Database platform convergence can preserve separate transactional and analytical engines. Storage integration and developer workflow can differentiate platforms even when their core query techniques are similar.

## Follow-Ups

- Seek a named case study for the bank example: question coverage, semantic-layer maintenance, rejected questions, evaluation leakage controls, failure severity and cost per correct answer. Compare like-for-like enterprise workflows rather than equating the anecdote with an aggregate benchmark such as [Spider 2.0](https://spider2-sql.github.io/).
- Request the workload, model, tuning objective and measured runtime behind Pavlo's 15-minute and 80–85% estimates. Reproduce against cloud defaults and specialized tuners with staging-to-production transfer and rollback included.
- Track agent-originated query volume, concurrency, branch lifetimes, retained data and monetization separately; distinguish first-party measurements from the 10–100× forecast.
- Follow a concrete ClickHouse GPU prototype or release. Compare cold and hot runs, data sizes beyond GPU memory, CPU fallbacks, transfer overhead and cost per query before applying Sirius results to ClickHouse.
- Compare shipped Postgres-to-analytics integration paths on freshness, operational isolation, storage duplication, recovery and total cost; separate LTAP architecture announcements from availability and customer results.
- Obtain the Database of Databases observation date and included repository denominator before using the reported 60% in a model; separate presence of signed AI commits from code volume, review burden and defect rates.
