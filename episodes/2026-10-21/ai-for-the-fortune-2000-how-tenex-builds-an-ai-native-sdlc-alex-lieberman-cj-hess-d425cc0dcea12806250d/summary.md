# AI for the Fortune 2000: How Tenex Builds an AI-Native SDLC — Alex Lieberman & CJ Hess

- Podcast: Latent Space
- Published: 2026-10-21
- Source: https://www.latent.space/p/tenex
- Relevance: 4/5

Tenex's Alex Lieberman and CJ Hess describe a 75-person applied-AI business whose customers are evenly split between Fortune 2000 companies and mid-market firms. Their most useful evidence concerns the gap between buying AI access and deploying production agents, the changing economics of paying engineers for output, and a shared engineering process that stores project decisions alongside code. The operating numbers are management estimates, not audited results: reported output growth is particularly hard to normalize because Tenex recalibrates its story-point definitions.

**Why it matters:** The interview makes the implementation bottleneck concrete: enterprises need agreed processes, access controls and durable project context before more capable agents translate into reliable work. For investors and operators, it also separates three economics that are often conflated: employee output, inference expense and the continuing cost of owning customized software.

- **Tenex identifies inconsistent business processes and data permissions as recurring blockers to background agents, and Lieberman says it has turned bespoke permissioning work into its own gateway product.** [22:08; 22:46; 23:22; 23:57] _agents_developer_tools; observation; medium confidence._ Lieberman describes CIO/CISO difficulty controlling which data individual assistants may read, then says Tenex productized a gateway after building customer-specific versions. - **Hess roughly places Tenex's raw-token spending at 1–2x employee spending, while reporting that a shift toward a cheaper sufficiently capable model has recently lowered costs substantially.** [14:53; 15:29; 16:07; 17:52; 18:30; 19:08] _companies_capital_allocation; observation; medium confidence._ The ratio is an off-the-cuff answer to swyx's comparison of token and employee expenditure; Hess explicitly lacks the number in front of him. These are competing theses and an early build-cost anecdote, not evidence of realized enterprise savings or declining vertical-AI revenue.

## Changed Views Or Tensions

- The strongest bottleneck evidence is the distance between distributing general AI tools and operating repeatable background workflows. Tenex's selected customer sample raises the importance of process definition and permissioning, without establishing a market-wide adoption rate.
- Paying for completed engineering work does not remove measurement problems: increasingly valuable work happens before code is written, and changing story-point definitions can obscure how much reported productivity has actually improved.
- Cheaper software construction should put the most pressure on replaceable task features and poorly fitting smaller tools. The discussion gives less support to blanket displacement of enterprise systems whose integration, migration and maintenance burdens remain substantial.

## Follow-Ups

- Ask Tenex for dated, consistently defined deployment cohorts behind the 90/60/50/5 adoption estimates, including how many prospects subsequently put non-engineering agents into production.
- Request quality-adjusted output, rework, customer retention and gross-margin data using a stable workload definition; separate model improvements, MetaHarness changes and story-point repricing.
- Clarify the token-to-employee spending ratio's period, denominator and treatment of subscriptions, then compare cost per accepted deliverable before and after the model switch.
- Track the gateway's actual customer deployments, permission boundaries and evaluation process, and check the SaaS replacement after its first production conference for maintenance effort and user outcomes.
