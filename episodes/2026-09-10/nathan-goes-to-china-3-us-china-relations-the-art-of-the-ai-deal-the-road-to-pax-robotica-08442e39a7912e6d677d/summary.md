# Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal & the Road to Pax Robotica

- Source: https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/nathan-goes-to-china-3-us-china-relations-the-art-of-the-ai-deal-the-road-to-pax-robotica/
- Relevance: 4/5

In this solo commentary following a two-week China visit, Nathan Labenz argues for a cooperative US-China AI framework rather than a race for decisive superiority. His most concrete contributions are a report that export-control uncertainty is deterring safety collaborations, a warning that divergent hardware and model architectures could make each side's capabilities harder to assess, and proposals for shared research compute, confidential audits, and reciprocal technology exchange. These are his observations and policy recommendations, not announced government agreements.

**Why it matters:** The episode identifies costs of technological separation beyond chip availability: interrupted safety research, loss of shared evaluation methods, and weaker confidence about the other side's capabilities. Its proposed alternatives connect semiconductor access, manufacturing know-how, deployment liability, and research transparency. The strongest material is the specific mechanism behind each recommendation; his informal conversations do not establish representative Chinese opinion, and the feasibility of the proposed safeguards remains unproven.

## Changed Views Or Tensions

- Export controls may have an operational cost outside the semiconductor supply chain: Labenz's conversations suggest uncertainty alone can discourage safety collaborations, independently of what the rules actually prohibit.
- Shared hardware, architectures, open weights, and research practices can function as a way to assess an international competitor's capabilities; losing that common basis could matter even if chip restrictions successfully delay some development.
- Model-access policy need not be framed only as unrestricted access versus national bans: insurance and auditable safeguards are alternative designs worth evaluating, although this episode does not establish their feasibility.

## Follow-Ups

- Seek primary regulatory guidance and documented organization-level examples to distinguish actual restrictions on safety collaboration from uncertainty or conservative compliance practice.
- Assess whether shared compute or hardware-verification pilots could preserve technical transparency without creating unacceptable security dependencies.
- Evaluate the confidential-audit proposal against incomplete logs, manipulated inputs, model error, output leakage, and the need to prove that an agreed process actually ran.
- Look for concrete insurer coverage terms and risk models before treating deployment insurance as a practical substitute for model restrictions.
- Track official bilateral statements separately from Labenz's proposed chip-for-manufacturing bargain and autonomous-weapons framework.
