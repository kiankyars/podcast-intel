# Beam: The Great American Open Model with ReflectionAI Co-Founder and CEO Misha Laskin

- Podcast: No Priors
- Published: 2026-10-09
- Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=up4sG9RM20M
- Relevance: 4/5

Reflection CEO Misha Laskin gives a concrete account of Beam's training economics: roughly 6,000 GB300s for pretraining versus more than 10,000 for four weeks of reinforcement learning. He explains why Reflection moved from adapting other labs' models to training its own base, how it intends to monetize enterprise inference infrastructure and deployment work, and why customer-specific evaluations and harnesses matter despite strong general agent capabilities. Beam's advertised efficiency needs careful interpretation, and public weights are still promised rather than available.

**Why it matters:** The interview makes the open-model business concrete: expensive RL produces a potentially efficient workhorse, but revenue depends on supplying compute, operating software and solving customer workflows. The useful investment distinction is between competitive benchmark capability, estimated generation efficiency and proven deployment economics. Laskin also identifies a deployment labor constraint: enterprises need people who can turn business tasks into evaluations and reliable agent systems.

He describes roughly 6,000 GB300s for several weeks of pretraining and more than 10,000 for four weeks of RL. His approximately 12-day pretraining figure describes what improved infrastructure could now achieve, not the original run's elapsed time. The [launch post](https://reflection.ai/blog/introducing-beam) specifies 6,144 GPUs for pretraining under four weeks and 10.5K for four weeks of RL, consistent with his rounded account. He reports continuing gains when training ended; this is an observation over that run, not evidence that scaling cannot saturate. The implication is a large post-training compute and agent-environment budget, rather than inexpensive fine-tuning of a finished base.
- **Beam's claimed 3–4× reasoning efficiency is a generation-compute comparison, not demonstrated 3–4× lower customer bills or latency.** [19:55; 19:59; 20:07; 20:13; 20:27; 20:31; 20:43; 20:49; 21:02; 21:40] _frontier_labs_models; observation; high confidence._ Laskin connects shorter agent solutions to lower cost and faster completion, reports 3–4× efficiency against similar-capability models and roughly 10× against some larger models, and credits a strong base plus large-scale RL. The [published methodology](https://reflection.ai/blog/introducing-beam) estimates forward-pass FLOPs as twice active parameters times generated tokens, including reasoning and answers; it excludes prompt prefill, context-dependent attention and serving overhead. Its headline 3–4× comparator is GLM-5.2, and the company acknowledges stronger open models on raw capability. This supports a promising efficiency hypothesis, not universal frontier leadership or measured production savings. Workload completion rate, hardware utilization and total serving cost still require customer tests; Laskin supplies no comparable deployment bill or wall-clock study here.

He says selling inference alone has not worked in his experience, and gives an on-premises Dell infrastructure scenario rather than a named customer's measured migration result. The [enterprise offering](https://reflection.ai/enterprise-solutions) likewise lists infrastructure, agent software and forward-deployed implementation. Availability needs qualification: [developer docs](https://developers.reflection.ai/introduction) describe a waitlisted API beta, while the [launch post](https://reflection.ai/blog/introducing-beam) promises Apache 2.0 weights later in October. Public self-hosting should therefore not be treated as already delivered. No revenue, margin, utilization or service-cost figures establish this business model's economics yet.
- **Laskin expects most enterprise open-model usage to start with customized agent systems, with model fine-tuning following later; he also reports incomplete generalization between agent harnesses.** [18:10; 18:22; 18:29; 19:14; 19:19; 19:22; 19:32; 27:16; 27:25; 27:29; 28:00; 28:14; 28:24; 28:44; 29:11] _agents_developer_tools; forecast; medium confidence._ He says capability does not transfer cleanly even across Terminal Bench versions and harnesses, although suitable data can make adaptation fast. His customer recipe starts with a useful task evaluation, then generates synthetic training data approximating that task; it is not automatically training on the customer's private records. For traditional enterprises he predicts adapting a harness around an unchanged open model before fine-tuning, because existing systems lack the native data-collection loops of AI-first products. He contrasts this with an unnamed inference providers' greater-than-90% customization statistic, which he explicitly limits to a customer mix dominated by AI-native companies; it is not a measured enterprise-wide rate. The practical implication is to test the actual workflow and harness before attributing a benchmark score to a deployable application. The adoption sequence remains his forecast, supported by customer conversations rather than a representative survey.
- **Laskin expects frontier research teams to remain in the hundreds while demand expands for deployment engineers who can build evaluations, collect data and adapt agent systems.** [01:37; 01:50; 11:29; 11:48; 11:51; 01:03:12; 01:03:30; 01:04:09; 01:04:39; 01:04:56; 01:05:03; 01:06:21; 01:07:54; 01:08:06; 01:08:21; 01:08:54; 01:09:01] _companies_capital_allocation; forecast; medium confidence._ He describes model assistance with hyperparameter searches and infrastructure details, but says scientific intuition and uneven capabilities still require people. He loosely estimates several-fold faster research progress; the interview's roughly 30× annual compute-efficiency claim lacks a fixed metric, baseline or auditable study, so it should not become a measured company productivity statistic. He stresses the staff-to-compute ratio, saying thousands of researchers would not necessarily help a single effort. Capability work currently uses pods of five to ten people building evaluations and data, and he argues enterprise deployment reproduces that need for many individual workflows. He says Reflection would hire as many engineers with those skills as it could find and identifies training them as the constraint. His forecast of a roughly hundreds-sized core research effort is distinct from the approximately 300 total employees reported at the opening.

## Changed Views Or Tensions

- Treat Reflection as a prospective full-stack enterprise inference supplier: open-model distribution and implementation work are intended to generate continuing compute demand, not just stand-alone model-license revenue.
- Beam's training account strengthens the view that a serious open reasoning model can require a larger RL compute campaign than pretraining; the base recipe, agent environments and inference infrastructure must work together.
- Separate the podcast's language of releasing an open model from actual public availability: as checked October 10, the official documentation still describes waitlisted access and weights promised later this month.
- A model's apparent intelligence and a working customer agent are different evidence levels: Laskin's harness-transfer caveat makes workflow-specific evaluations central to deployment assessment.
